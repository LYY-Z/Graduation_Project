# Adaptive Robot Motion Control Literature Review

Presenter notes for seven main slides and six appendices. Bracketed text gives pointing cues and is not read aloud.

## Slide 1

TAM: Torque-level dynamics adaptation

[Introduce the research goal, then point to the central figure from left to right.]

This survey focuses on how robots can recover their intended motion when payload, friction or actuator response changes during operation. I compare the research questions, control interfaces, experimental evidence and public reproduction conditions, with Go2-W and UR7e as our target platforms.

The first paper is TAM, presented at CoRL 2026 as a Spotlight. Its question is how to preserve a policy’s intended motion when the real robot’s dynamics differ from the reference dynamics.

[Point to the policy, the controller and then the pink TAM block.]

On the left, the policy can come from reinforcement learning, behavior cloning or MPC. The controller converts the policy action into nominal joint torques. TAM sits between this controller and the robot. It adds a residual torque so that the actual response more closely matches the intended reference response.

[Point first to the history encoder and then to the torque adaptor.]

The Transformer encodes the history of joint motion and applied torques to estimate the current dynamics mismatch. A small MLP uses this estimate and the current control information to produce the correction for each joint. At deployment, adaptation occurs through the history representation rather than online network-weight updates.

[Point to the training text and the input/output summary.]

TAM itself is mainly trained through supervised learning in simulation. The ideal and perturbed models provide the torque-correction targets. The reference model also helps construct a physics-residual feature from past motion.

Its contribution is to place adaptation at the torque interface, allowing different upstream policies to reuse the same type of correction mechanism. The inputs are robot states, torque history and nominal controller torque. The output is a vector of per-joint residual torques. The next slide examines whether the experiments support this policy reuse.

## Slide 2

TAM: Policy reuse and adaptation to sudden changes

[Compare the two rows on the left, then move across the four task columns.]

The upper row shows execution without TAM, and the lower row shows execution with TAM. The four Panda hardware settings are RL box pushing, behavior-cloned cube flipping, MPC ball balancing and trajectory tracking with a payload. Together, they test reuse across different upstream policies and control objectives.

The metrics are task-specific. Pushing and flipping use success rate, ball balancing uses goals reached within a time limit, and trajectory tracking uses RMSE. For pushing, success rises from 47.6% to 76.2%, an increase of 28.6 percentage points.

[Point to the baseline line below the result.]

The main comparisons are Direct execution and Online SysID, which estimates physical parameters before computing a correction. Online SysID reaches 57.1% pushing success. RMA is evaluated only for pushing, while payload tracking also includes PhysPred. These baselines should not be treated as a single common set across every task.

[Point to time zero on the right-hand plot, then to the vertical axis.]

This simulation changes dynamics parameters during execution. Zero on the horizontal axis is the change time. The vertical axis is joint-position RMSE, so lower is better. The gray Direct curve remains high, while the TAM variants reduce the error after the change.

Under the paper’s recovery threshold, the original model takes about 4.6 seconds, and DAgger fine-tuning reduces this to about 1.7 seconds. DAgger exposes TAM to histories generated by its own corrections, while keeping the upstream policy fixed. These times apply to this specific tracking protocol.

The paper also evaluates seven robot arms in simulation and includes history, physics-residual and OOD-friction ablations. The official release provides training, checkpoints and representative evaluations. UR7e still requires model and torque-interface extensions; Go2-W additionally requires floating-base and contact-dynamics extensions. Reference-model assumptions, training coverage and actuator capacity remain important limitations. GRAM addresses adaptation at a different level, inside the policy.

## Slide 3

GRAM: Adaptation guided by confidence

[Start with the problem, then follow the four boxes across the top.]

GRAM was published in RA-L 2026. It addresses a difficulty with adaptation itself: history-based inference can work well in familiar conditions but become unreliable outside the training distribution. Purely robust behavior, however, may be unnecessarily conservative in familiar conditions.

The method reads state–action history, estimates context and uncertainty, conditions a unified policy, and outputs joint-position targets.

[Point to the multiple prediction branches in the original diagram.]

These branches represent different context estimates for the same history. Larger disagreement indicates lower confidence. The fusion coefficient alpha shifts the representation toward the inferred context when confidence is high, and toward a learned robust representation when confidence is low.

[Point to the ID and OOD illustrations at the bottom of the figure.]

The distinction is between in-distribution and out-of-distribution conditions. Fusion occurs in the conditioning representation before one policy produces actions. It is not a direct average of two controllers’ actions, and it does not require a known fault label.

[Point to the training text on the right.]

PPO trains the motion policy. Teacher–student learning trains history-based context inference using conditions known in simulation, and adversarial training develops robust behavior. At deployment, new history changes the internal representation while network weights remain fixed.

Inputs include joint and base states, motion commands and history. For the original Go2, the policy produces 12 joint-position targets for the low-level controller. Unlike TAM’s torque correction, this is policy-level adaptation. The next slide examines how it balances adaptation and robustness experimentally.

## Slide 4

GRAM: Success under challenging hardware conditions

[Read the three photo rows from top to bottom.]

The difficult hardware scenarios are a nine-kilogram payload, a slippery surface, and a transition from a ramp to foam. These cover both load and contact changes. Entering the slippery surface or foam also changes the dynamics during a trial.

[Point to the three success counts on the right.]

Each method has five trials per scenario, giving 15 trials per method. GRAM succeeds in all 15, Robust RL in 12, and Contextual RL in two. Contextual RL relies on inferred conditions, whereas Robust RL emphasizes performance under difficult conditions. This comparison supports the value of checking inference confidence when the conditions are unfamiliar.

The paper also tests nominal operation, a frozen rear hip and uphill/downhill motion. Across seven controlled scenarios, three methods and five repetitions, there are 105 trials. All methods succeed on the moderate scenarios, so the strongest differences appear in the difficult cases.

Simulation separates ID, near-OOD and far-OOD conditions and compares normalized return against Robust RL, Contextual RL and domain randomization. GRAM is not best in every cell. Its strength is the balance between familiar and unfamiliar conditions. Ablations examine the unified policy and joint training.

One experiment adds nine kilograms after ten seconds, but primarily measures the fusion coefficient’s response. It should not be described as a complete recovery-time benchmark. The outdoor tests are mainly qualitative.

[Point to the reproduction and limitations lines.]

Official Go2 training, adaptation, evaluation and baseline code are available. This makes GRAM a practical starting point for the wheeled-legged direction. However, 15 successes do not establish recovery under every disturbance, and neither Go2-W nor robot arms is validated in the paper. SADL takes another route by improving prediction before replanning actions.

## Slide 5

SADL: Situation-aware dynamics and planning

[Introduce the question, then point from Environment back to the red identification block.]

SADL was published in IJRR 2026. It asks why the same action produces different motion under different hidden terrain or dynamics conditions, and how this information can improve planning. It is a model-based learning and control approach.

After each action, the robot observes a state transition. The red module detects changes in the response and constructs a situation representation. It does not require manually supplied labels such as grass or mud. Situations are inferred from interaction feedback.

[Point to the green transition model and then Evaluate & Select.]

The dynamics model combines the state, candidate action and situation representation to predict future motion. The planner compares candidate actions, executes a selected action and receives new feedback. The ground-vehicle experiments use MPPI. The contribution is to condition dynamics learning on hidden situations, reducing errors caused by pooling incompatible responses.

[Point to the Jackal platform photo.]

The learning-efficiency study compares model-based methods, including PETS and TD-MPC2, with model-free RL methods such as PPO, SAC and TD3. Navigation experiments instead mainly compare the Dubins model and probabilistic ensembles with different inputs. The learning baselines are not all repeated as hardware baselines.

In mixed-terrain Jackal hardware tests, each method has ten runs. SADL succeeds in ten, Dubins in seven, and the other two models in four and zero. This supports improved navigation success, not superiority on every speed or smoothness metric.

[Point to the quadrotor wind-simulation label.]

The paper also tests gate crossing under three switching-wind configurations and provides surface-vessel situation analysis. These studies support applications across domains, but do not demonstrate one set of weights controlling all robots.

[Point to the bottom limitation.]

The situation library is built during training and fixed during testing. The method therefore does not establish unlimited online learning of arbitrary new failures. The official release includes core components and a QuadX example, but a complete vehicle-hardware reproduction package is not confirmed. Both target platforms require response-model and planning-interface extensions. The next slide places the other references in context.

## Slide 6

Additional references: Venues and research roles

[Move down the table, emphasizing Research focus and Role in our study.]

I retained four additional papers as baselines or methodological references.

ESCP studies short-history representations and sudden environment changes, making it a relevant adaptation comparison. RMA² is more focused on manipulation and combines visual information with interaction history. UARL studies uncertainty-aware residual learning, which can inform correction learning on top of an existing policy.

RecoveryChaining returns a robot from failure states to states where the original task can continue. This is related to recovery, but its problem definition differs from continuous compensation under unknown dynamics.

These papers appeared at AAAI, CVPR, RA-L and IROS. Conferences and journals should be identified separately. Venue reputation is useful context, but selection still depends on the problem, evidence and control interfaces. The seven papers are therefore not seven parallel reproduction projects. The final slide summarizes the three main candidates.

## Slide 7

Selection: Reproduction scope and transfer effort

[Compare the three rows before moving across the two platform columns.]

The three methods operate at different control levels. GRAM adapts the policy’s conditioning representation, TAM corrects execution torques, and SADL identifies situations before prediction and planning. Success rates from their different tasks cannot be used as a direct ranking.

GRAM provides a Go2 training and evaluation workflow. TAM provides training, checkpoints and example evaluations. SADL provides core models and a QuadX example. These support original-task reproduction, but do not establish that every paper experiment can be reproduced directly.

[Point to the Go2-W and UR7e columns.]

GRAM is closest to the current Go2-W locomotion task, although wheel actions and retraining are required. UR7e would require a manipulation task, reward design and policy training. Go2 weights cannot directly control the arm.

TAM is closer to UR7e’s manipulation setting, requiring a reference model and torque interface. Go2-W additionally introduces floating-base and wheel–ground contact dynamics. SADL needs a response model and planner at the selected control level. Interface compatibility is necessary, but training and validation are still required.

[Follow the three steps at the bottom.]

The proposed sequence is original-task reproduction, platform extension and sudden-change evaluation. GRAM is the initial candidate for validation on the wheeled-legged platform, with the cost of extending it to manipulation assessed separately. Evaluation should emphasize changes during an episode, measuring recovery time, cumulative tracking error and failure rate rather than only fixed unknown parameters.

TAM and SADL remain alternatives at other control levels. A research contribution should come from a specific failure mechanism, rather than platform transfer alone. This concludes the main presentation. The appendices provide the publication and experiment details for discussion.

## Appendix A0

Appendix A0: Publication venues

[Point to Venue and Type.]

All seven papers have formal publication venues, but they are not all journal papers. TAM is a CoRL conference paper with a Spotlight presentation. GRAM and UARL appeared in RA-L, and SADL in IJRR. The remaining papers appeared at AAAI, CVPR and IROS.

IJRR is a leading robotics journal, and RA-L is a high-quality robotics journal. Conference papers should be described using their conference venues. For selection, I also consider interfaces, experiment coverage and public code. Venue alone does not determine suitability for our research.

## Appendix A1

Appendix A1: TAM experiments and baselines

[Point to the first four rows, then the last two.]

The first four rows cover Panda hardware tasks, examining reuse across policies, task performance and payload tracking. The last two are simulation studies of cross-robot tracking and mid-episode dynamics changes.

Baselines vary by task. Direct and Online SysID are the main comparisons. RMA is used only for pushing, while PhysPred is included in payload tracking. Pushing and flipping use success rate, balancing counts goals, tracking measures RMSE, and the sudden-change experiment also measures recovery time. These metrics support distinct conclusions and should not be aggregated into a single score.

## Appendix A2

Appendix A2: TAM supplementary validation

[Point to the first three rows, then nominal-condition impact and oscillation analysis.]

The first three analyses examine the roles of history, physics-residual inputs and latent dimension. The remaining tests ask whether TAM harms an already matched controller, how it behaves beyond the training friction range, and whether it introduces oscillations.

The paper therefore checks both task benefits and potential side effects. These tests still cover finite conditions and do not guarantee stability under arbitrary contact or failures.

## Appendix A3

Appendix A3: GRAM experiment coverage

[Read the table from top to bottom.]

Simulation compares ID, near-OOD and far-OOD conditions using normalized return. It then varies the training mass distribution to test dependence on randomization coverage. The nine-kilogram load change after ten seconds mainly examines the response of the fusion coefficient.

Hardware evaluation contains seven controlled scenarios, three methods and five repetitions, totaling 105 trials. It measures goal-reaching success and completion time. Outdoor results are mainly qualitative. Recovery time, cumulative error after sudden changes and repeated disturbances remain useful targets for a dedicated evaluation.

## Appendix A4

Appendix A4: SADL learning and mechanism analysis

[Point to learning efficiency, then the mechanism-analysis rows.]

The first study compares learning efficiency in vehicle simulation using both model-based approaches and model-free RL baselines. SADL learns dynamics and uses planning to select actions.

Input ablations, change-point detection and feature analysis examine why situation representations help. The final test applies the same action in 14 environments, collecting 250 transitions per environment to show that different conditions produce different responses. These are prediction and mechanism results, distinct from navigation success.

## Appendix A5

Appendix A5: SADL navigation and cross-domain tests

[Point to navigation first, then quadrotor and surface-vessel studies.]

Navigation evaluation includes four simulated terrain levels and mixed-terrain Jackal hardware tests, using success, waypoints, time and smoothness metrics. The difficult slope is primarily compared against Dubins, while other outdoor demonstrations are qualitative.

Quadrotor evaluation uses switching-wind simulation to compare SA-PETS with PETS. The surface-vessel study adds situation-detection analysis. These examples support use across domains, but each requires its own state/action definition and dynamics model. They do not establish direct support for Go2-W and UR7e.
